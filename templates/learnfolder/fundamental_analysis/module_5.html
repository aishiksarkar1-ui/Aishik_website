Module 5: Political and Macroeconomic Factors

Just as a person cannot live in isolation, no business operates in a vacuum. Even a company with fine managers and a wonderful product is heavily influenced by external forces that are often completely beyond its control.

To accurately evaluate a stock, a fundamental analyst must look at the broader political and macroeconomic landscape. Here are the major external factors that drive the markets:

1. Political Stability

A stable political environment is absolutely necessary for steady, balanced economic growth.

Why it matters: If a country is ruled by a stable government that makes decisions for long-term development, industries and companies will naturally prosper. On the other hand, political instability creates widespread insecurity. The mere possibility of a government being ousted and replaced by one with diametrically opposite economic beliefs is enough to scare away major investors.

2. Foreign Exchange (Forex) Reserves

A country needs an adequate stockpile of foreign currencies (primarily the US Dollar) to pay for its imports, service its foreign debts, and meet international commitments.

The 1991 Crisis Example: Without enough foreign exchange, a country loses international confidence and cannot import the materials necessary for its development. For example, in 1991, India faced a massive economic crisis when its forex reserves dropped to just $532 million—barely enough to cover a few weeks of imports. India was forced to devalue the rupee, pledge gold, and take an IMF loan to survive. Today, strong forex reserves act as a massive safety net for the economy.

3. Crude Oil Prices

Because oil powers the global supply chain, crude oil prices are a critical macroeconomic indicator.

  • The Ripple Effect: When oil prices skyrocket, it directly increases the cost of all petroleum products and indirectly drives up the price of almost every other key product due to higher transportation and manufacturing costs.
  • What drives the price? Oil prices are highly volatile and react to global demand and supply, world events like wars or dramatic weather changes, the value of the US dollar, and output decisions made by the OPEC country group.

4. The Dual Nature of Inflation

Inflation is the sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services, which results in a decrease in your currency's purchasing power.

  • How it works: If inflation is at 3%, an item that previously cost Rs. 100 will now cost Rs. 103. Simply put, your Rs. 100 note can now only buy 97% of the goods it used to.
  • Is it always bad? While many people think inflation is terrible, a moderate inflation rate of 3% to 4% is generally considered good because it implies the economy is growing and consumer demand is strong. However, uncontrolled, unanticipated inflation is dangerous because it destroys the purchasing power of people on fixed incomes and hurts economic growth.

5. Interest Rates and Taxation

Government and central bank policies act as the accelerator and brakes of the economy.

  • Interest Rates: Regulated by the central bank (like the RBI in India), interest rates are heavily tied to the money supply and inflation. When inflation is dangerously high, the central bank raises interest rates to make borrowing expensive, which cools down spending. Conversely, to push a depressed economy, they lower rates to encourage consumer spending and investment.
  • Taxation: The level of taxation directly affects the economy. If tax rates are kept relatively low, people have more disposable income and a greater incentive to work hard and invest. Interestingly, economic data shows that tax collection is generally highest when rates are between 30% and 55%; pushing rates higher than this can actually cause total tax collections to decline.
