Module 2: The Three-Part Analysis (The EIC Framework)

When analyzing a business, it is easy to get tunnel vision and only look at the company's own financial statements. However, a company does not exist in a vacuum. Its success is heavily influenced by the world around it.

In fundamental analysis, the most important factor is information—specifically, any information that can affect the growth and profitability of a company. Because of this, fundamental analysis is structurally broken down into three distinct parts: the economy, the industry, and the company itself.

This layered approach is often called the EIC Framework (Economy, Industry, Company), and it acts like a funnel, guiding you from the biggest macroeconomic picture down to the finest micro-level details.

Here is how the three parts work together:

1. Economic Analysis (The Macro Environment)

Before looking at a specific stock, you must understand the broader economic environment. This involves looking at national and global factors like GDP growth, interest rates, inflation, and government policies.

Why it matters: If the overall economy is sinking, even the best-run companies will struggle to stay afloat.

Example: Imagine you want to invest in a luxury car manufacturer. If the country is going through a severe recession and unemployment is high, people will not have the disposable income to buy luxury cars. In this case, the broader economic conditions make it a risky time to invest, regardless of how good the cars are.

2. Industry Analysis (The Sector)

Once you understand the economy, the next step is to evaluate the specific industry or sector in which the company operates. You need to assess if the industry is growing, stagnant, or declining, and understand the level of competition.

Why it matters: A booming economy won't save a dying industry.

Example: Suppose the overall economy is thriving. People have money, and businesses are spending. However, you are analyzing a company that manufactures traditional physical DVDs. Because the world has shifted to digital streaming (like Netflix and Amazon Prime), the DVD industry as a whole is shrinking. Even in a great economy, this specific industry is facing a massive downturn.

3. Company Analysis (The Specific Business)

The final and most detailed layer is analyzing the specific company. Now that you know the economy is healthy and the industry is growing, you must find out if this particular company is a winner. This involves looking at their balance sheets, management team, debt levels, and competitive advantages.

Why it matters: A growing industry will have many competitors, but only a few will be highly profitable market leaders.

Example: The economy is booming, and the Electric Vehicle (EV) industry is experiencing massive, rapid growth. Now you look at "Company X," an EV manufacturer. You dive into their company specifics and find out they have terrible management, massive amounts of debt, and a history of delaying product launches. Despite the great economy and the hot industry, Company X is a poor investment.

Bringing It All Together

By gathering information across all three of these distinct parts, an investor can properly interpret and analyze the data to determine the true intrinsic value of a share. Once this intrinsic value is determined, it must be compared against the current market value to make a sound, logical investment decision.
